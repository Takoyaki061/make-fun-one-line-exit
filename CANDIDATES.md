# Candidates — The One-Line Exit

Judge criteria (most important first): (1) a funny one-line resignation
letter whose joke lands; (2) surprise — the punchline arrives somewhere
unexpected; (3) fits the form — one line, no padding, reads like a letter
someone could actually send; (4) specific voice — a recognizable job or
workplace; (5) stays with the reader — quotable, forwardable.

Gate: the text must be 1–140 UTF-8 characters, one line.

## Candidates (DeepSeek brutal brainstorm, 6 lines, 2 micro-call batches)

1. Lifeguard — "I've decided to stop watching people drown professionally and start doing it recreationally—consider this my two weeks' notice, effective immediately." (~160 chars — REJECTED, over the 140-char gate.)
2. IT support — "Have you tried turning your company off and on again? Because I did, and I'm not coming back." (93 chars)
3. Zookeeper — "The animals and I have agreed to a mutual separation—they keep the enclosure, I keep my dignity, and the penguins keep the keys." (128 chars)
4. Wedding DJ — "I'm quitting — I can't keep playing Celebration while watching a marriage end at the dessert table." (99 chars)
5. Night-shift security guard — "I'm resigning — the only thing I've secured in six months is a lasting fear of hallway mirrors." (95 chars)
6. Barista — "I quit — I've steamed so much milk I now hear latte art in my dreams, and the swan is judging me." (97 chars)

## Critique-pick

DeepSeek's verdict: **#3 (zookeeper)** — "the escalating absurdity lands
perfectly; 'penguins keep the keys' is an unexpected, vivid punchline."

My own critique agrees:

- #2 (IT support) is the most instantly recognizable joke and the most
  quotable, but its twist is the most predictable — every reader sees the
  "turn it off and on again" turn coming, so it scores lower on surprise
  (criterion 2), which the judge weighs above voice and quotability.
- #3 wins on surprise: the line opens as dry HR language ("mutual
  separation"), then escalates through three clauses, each funnier than the
  last, and the punchline — the penguins keep the keys — is the one detail
  nobody can predict. It also has the most specific voice (a zookeeper
  negotiating with his animals) and reads exactly like a letter someone
  could send, if only to a very strange HR department.
- #4 (DJ) is a strong second: a sharp image, but the joke is a single
  observation rather than a built punchline, and it leans on the reader
  knowing the song.
- #5 (guard) is funny but the punchline ("fear of hallway mirrors") is a
  mood, not a turn — it describes the job rather than resigning from it
  with a twist.
- #6 (barista) is surreal in a good way, but "the swan is judging me"
  drifts away from resignation-letter form; it reads as a complaint, not
  a letter.

**Picked: #3, the zookeeper.** 128 characters, one line, under the gate.
